## Problem

This is a 45-minute competency interview at the final-interview stage of the process, run either by a
people-and-culture partner or by another interviewer trained on the "Googleyness and leadership" rubric. It
checks whether the pattern that carried you through the technical rounds also holds for how you work with
people, take ownership under ambiguity, and respond under pressure, rather than re-testing technical skill.
Most questions ask for one real situation from your own past, not a hypothetical, and the interviewer
expects an answer structured as Situation, Task, Action, Result (STAR); a follow-up probe pushes past the
headline of the story for your own specific actions, the evidence that they had the effect you claim, and
what you took from it afterward. A few ask for a stated answer or position rather than a story — the
mission, AGI, your long-term plan, most of the leadership and teams group — and expect it backed by one
concrete example.
"Why Google DeepMind" returns here with more depth than the version of
that question asked at the recruiter screen. The questions fall into six groups.

### Motivation and mission

- Why Google DeepMind, and why this team, at this point in your career — tie the answer to one decision you
  have already made because of it, not a repeat of what you said at the recruiter screen.
- Tell me about a time your own day-to-day work was shaped by what building AI "safely and responsibly"
  means to you — a decision it changed, not a belief you hold in the abstract.
- Describe one piece of Google DeepMind's public work — a launch, a paper, a policy choice — you would have
  done differently, and say what you would change and why.
- Tell me about a time you turned down a more prestigious or better-paid opportunity because it did not fit
  what you actually wanted to work on.
- What is the one condition that would make you leave, once you are here?
- What is Google DeepMind's mission?
- Do you think AGI is possible? What would change your mind?
- What is your long-term plan — where do you want to be in five to ten years (for a student: after
  graduating)?

### Collaboration and conflict

- Tell me about a disagreement with a researcher or a tech lead over priorities — what was each of you
  arguing for, and how was it resolved?
- Tell me about a time a teammate took sole credit for work you had done together — what did you do?
- Describe a time you helped a new teammate integrate into a team whose background or working style was
  very different from their own.
- Tell me about a research or technical disagreement that was resolved by evidence rather than by seniority
  or opinion — whose evidence, and what changed as a result?
- Tell me about a project that required you to work closely across research, engineering and product —
  where did those groups want different things, and how did you bridge it?

### Ownership, priorities and ambiguity

- Tell me about a time you were handed more work than you could do, and everyone insisted their piece was
  the highest priority — what did you actually do?
- Describe a project where the goal itself was unclear, or kept changing under you — how did you keep
  making progress against a target that would not hold still?
- Tell me about a time you had to ship something quickly without letting its quality drop — what did you
  actually cut, and what did you refuse to cut?
- Tell me about a time you noticed a teammate was overwhelmed and did something about it, even though it
  was not formally your job.
- Describe a piece of work that fell into the gap between two teams or roles, that nobody owned — what did
  you do about it?

### Leadership and teams

- What qualities does a leader need?
- What is the difference between management and leadership?
- Tell me about a time you worked to a tight deadline.
- What makes a good team?
- Should you be friends with your colleagues?

### Failure and feedback

- What is your biggest professional disappointment, and what changed in how you work because of it?
- Tell me about a mistake you made that affected other people — what happened, and how did you make it
  right?
- Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to someone more senior than you — what did you say, and
  what happened afterward?
- Tell me about a piece of feedback you received that was hard to hear, and how you acted on it.

### Responsibility

- Tell me about a time you raised a concern about the risk, potential misuse, or unfairness of something
  you were building — who did you raise it with, and what happened?
- Describe a time you had to weigh shipping on schedule against finishing a safety or privacy review — how
  did you decide, and who else was party to that decision?
- What would you do if, a month after shipping, you learned that a result or a conclusion you had published
  or launched was wrong?
- Tell me about a time you were the only person in the room raising an objection — what did you do?

## Reference solution

<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

Plan for four to six stories across the 45 minutes, at three to five minutes each: enough to cover the
interviewer's opening question and one or two follow-up probes, without so much detail that a single story
eats the time meant for the next question. The interviewer is listening for a consistent pattern across
stories more than for any single one being flawless, so reusing a story for two different questions because
it genuinely answers both costs you less than stretching a thin story to fill five minutes.

### The STAR skeleton

Budget each story to about two and a half to three minutes, leaving the remainder of its
three-to-five-minute slot for a probe. Situation and Task together take about 30 seconds: who was involved,
what you were trying to achieve, and why it mattered, compressed to the facts the Action needs and no
further backstory. Action takes 90 to 120 seconds and carries the actual content of the answer: narrate it
in "I" statements rather than "we", and name the two or three decision points where you chose one path over
another and why, since those choice points are what a follow-up probe returns to. Result takes about 30
seconds: a number, or a concrete, observable outcome that someone other than you could confirm — not "it
went well". Close with one sentence of what you learned or would do differently, stated as something you
have since applied, not a moral appended for the interviewer's benefit.

A result stated only as "it went well" or "the team was happy with it," with no number and no outcome a
third party could confirm, is the single most common gap across every group below. Fix: for each story in
your bank, write the Result sentence first, before the rest of the story, and set the story aside if you
cannot fill that sentence with a number or a concretely observable outcome.

### Motivation and mission

What it probes: whether your reason for choosing Google DeepMind is grounded in something you have actually
done, not restated from the recruiter screen or from the organisation's own public description of itself.

Skeleton: for "why Google DeepMind, why this team," name the one decision — turning down another offer,
changing your research direction, moving city — that you have already made because of this specific choice,
in one sentence, then connect it to what you would do in the role. For "safely and responsibly," use the
full STAR skeleton on a real instance: the situation where a shortcut was available, the action you
actually took instead, and the result, even a small one — a review you insisted on, an edge case you
refused to ship past. For the piece of public work you would change, name it specifically, state what you
would change in one sentence, and give the reason, distinct from simply disliking the outcome. For turning
down a more prestigious or better-paid option, name what you chose instead and why it mattered more than
the title or the number. For the mission question, give the wording briefly and verbatim — "to build AI
responsibly to benefit humanity" — then spend the rest of the answer on the one thing it changes about how
you work, rather than on the wording itself. For AGI, give a
position and your reasons for holding it, name the one piece of evidence that would change your mind, and
say what either answer implies for how you would work — treat it as a question about your own judgement,
not a prediction contest. For the long-term plan, name a direction stated as a capability or a kind of
impact you want to have, not a title or a level, and, if you are a student, answer for the period straight
after graduating instead.

Most common way to lose points: a motivation answer built from the same general sentences — ambitious
mission, important work — that would fit any AI lab, with no story attached to it. Fix: before the
interview, write down the one decision that is evidence of your interest and the one real instance of the
safety question, so a specific story comes out rather than a restatement of belief.

### Collaboration and conflict

What it probes: whether you can describe a real disagreement without turning the other person into an
obstacle, and whether your own actions in it are stated precisely enough to be checked.

Skeleton: name the other person's position as something a reasonable person could hold, not a strawman;
state your own position and the one piece of evidence or reasoning that moved the disagreement toward a
resolution; name the actual outcome, and say so plainly if it went the other person's way. For the credit
question, state the action you actually took — raised it with the teammate directly, brought it to a
manager, decided to let it go — and the real consequence, without narrating the teammate's motives as a
villain's. For working across research, engineering and product, name the concrete thing each side wanted
that the others did not, not a generic "alignment was hard".

Most common way to lose points: a conflict story where the other person is unreasonable throughout and you
are simply right, which reads as one-sided no matter how the facts actually were. Fix: before the
interview, state the other person's position in one sentence you would be comfortable saying to their face,
and check that your story still holds up once it is stated that fairly.

### Ownership, priorities and ambiguity

What it probes: how you actually behave once priority alone cannot resolve a conflict between two pieces of
work — the reasoning you used, not just the outcome.

Skeleton: for competing "high priority" work, name the criterion you actually used to choose — impact,
reversibility, who was blocked on it — and who you told about the piece you deprioritised, since silently
dropping something reads differently from flagging the trade-off. For an unclear or shifting goal, name the
one thing you kept fixed while the target moved, and how you found out the goal had changed. For shipping
at speed, name the specific thing you cut in one sentence and the specific thing you refused to cut and why.
For unowned work, state what made you pick it up rather than wait for someone to be assigned to it, and
what you did once it had a name attached.

Most common way to lose points: a priority story that ends at "I raised it with my manager and we figured
it out," which answers who made the call but not what you personally argued for. Fix: before the interview,
name the actual criterion behind each ownership or priority story in one sentence, so the reasoning is
stated rather than deferred to someone else's decision.

### Leadership and teams

What it probes: whether your opinions about leadership and teams come from something you have actually
done, and whether you can tell management and leadership apart in practice rather than only in theory.

Skeleton for the opinion questions — leader qualities, a good team, friends with colleagues — in about a
minute each: a clear position in one sentence, one concrete example from your own experience that shows it,
the trade-off or the limit of the position, and what you do because of it. For management versus
leadership, draw the distinction crisply: management is planning, resourcing, process, and accountability
for delivery through a formal role; leadership is setting direction and earning followers, which needs no
title — then give one example of leading without formal authority. For the tight deadline, use the full
STAR skeleton above. For friends with colleagues, give a balanced answer: trust and candour between friends
help the work, but keep decisions that affect them — reviews, credit, disagreements — independent of the
friendship.

Most common way to lose points: a platitude with no example behind it — a position anyone would agree with,
stated with nothing from your own history to back it up. Fix: before the interview, attach the one story
from your own experience to each opinion question, so the position arrives already anchored rather than
needing to be defended on the spot.

### Failure and feedback

What it probes: a real, specific cost you can name, and whether you would tell the same story if the
interviewer had not asked for a positive spin.

Skeleton: for the biggest disappointment, name the actual cost — to a project, to a person, to your own
trajectory — and the one concrete change in how you work since, checkable rather than a stated intention.
For a mistake that affected others, separate what went wrong from what you did about it: name who was
affected, the amends you actually made, and whether you were the one who surfaced the mistake or someone
else found it first. For feedback given to someone more senior, name the actual substance of what you said
and the result, including if it went badly. For feedback received, name the specific change you made
afterward that someone else could point to.

Most common way to lose points: a failure that is a disguised strength — "I worked too hard" or "I cared
too much" — which answers a different question than the one asked. Fix: pick a failure with a real,
nameable cost, and check that the lesson you state is something you can point to a later instance of
actually doing differently, not a sentiment.

### Responsibility

What it probes: whether you have actually raised a concern in a situation where it cost you something to
do so, not merely whether you would in principle.

Skeleton: name the specific concern — a misuse case, a fairness issue, a risk to someone not in the room —
and who you raised it with; state plainly what happened to the concern afterward, including if it was not
acted on, rather than implying it was resolved simply because you raised it. For speed against a safety or
privacy review, name the actual deadline pressure, the decision you made or argued for, and who else had to
sign off on it. For a shipped result turning out wrong, describe the concrete first step — who you would
tell, in what order — rather than a general commitment to transparency.

Most common way to lose points: a responsibility story with no real stakes — a concern that cost you
nothing to raise and was obviously going to be welcomed — which does not distinguish you from someone who
has never had to choose. Fix: pick an instance where raising the concern carried a real cost or a real
chance of being overruled, and say plainly what actually happened, including if the answer was no.

### Questions worth asking back

- What does the "Googleyness and leadership" rubric weigh most heavily for this particular role or level?
- How does the panel resolve it when interviewers disagree with each other?
- What is one thing this team wishes new joiners understood sooner about how it actually works day to day?
- What is the timeline from this round to a decision?

### When you have no story for a question

If nothing in your history matches a question as asked, say so directly rather than stretching an unrelated
story to fit — a forced fit is easier for an interviewer to spot than an honest gap, and a fabricated
situation that unravels under a probe costs you more than the gap would have. Offer the closest real
adjacent situation, name plainly how it differs from what was asked, and answer that one instead. If the
gap itself is informative — you have not yet had to give difficult feedback to someone senior because your
roles so far have not put you in that position — say so as part of the answer, rather than inventing a
moment that did not happen.
