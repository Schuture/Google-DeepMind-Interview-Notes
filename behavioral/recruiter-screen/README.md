## Problem

This is the first round of the process: a 30-minute video or phone call with a recruiter, sometimes joined
by a team lead. It decides whether the process continues, and toward which team or track. The recruiter
lays out the stages ahead — a few skills interviews, then final interviews with team leads and leadership,
then a decision — explains that the interviews take a competency-based approach, for which an answer
structured as Situation, Task, Action, Result (STAR) works well, and sends a non-disclosure agreement to
sign before the technical rounds begin. The questions fall into four groups.

### Motivation

- Why Google DeepMind specifically — not just an AI lab working on frontier models, but this one, rather
  than another lab doing similar work, or a product team elsewhere at Google?
- Why are you looking for a new role right now, and why leave the one you are in?
- Point to one piece of Google DeepMind's recent public work — a model release, a paper, a science result —
  that you have actually followed, and say what you think of it.
- What is Google DeepMind's mission, in its own words, and where does it figure in your own motivation for
  applying?
- If you end up with more than one offer, what decision framework would you use to choose between them?

### Research interests and team fit

- Which research area or product area do you want to work on, and why that one specifically?
- Research scientist, research engineer, or software engineer: which ladder are you applying for, and why
  does it fit how you like to work?
- Name a paper you admired recently that is not your own, and say what stayed with you about it.
- If the team you are ultimately matched to is not the one you applied for, are you open to that, and under
  what conditions?
- What would make a team a poor fit for you, even if its research area is one you find interesting?

### Background

- Walk through your background in about two minutes.
- Which of your past projects is most relevant to this role, and what was your own part in it end to end?
- How much hands-on experience do you have with the kind of work this role does day to day?
- If you are finishing a PhD: what is your thesis about, explained without jargon, and when do you expect
  to graduate?
- Is there a publication, an open-source project, or another piece of public work you would like the team
  to look at before the next round?

### Logistics

- What is your work-authorisation status, and would you need visa sponsorship?
- Which office locations can you work from, and what are your thoughts on the hybrid expectations for this
  role?
- When could you start? For students: what is your internship window, or your expected graduation date?
- What other interview processes are you in, at what stage, and do any of them carry a decision deadline?
- What is your current level, and what are your compensation expectations?
- What language do you want to use in the interviews, and is there an accommodation you need?

## Reference solution

<details>
<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

Four groups share thirty minutes, so treat them unequally on purpose. Motivation and research interests are
where a generic answer costs you the most — give them the most rehearsed, most specific material.
Background and logistics are mostly fact-finding: answer them in short, complete sentences and move on,
which leaves time for the recruiter's own explanations and for the questions you ask back at the end.

### Motivation

What it probes: whether the reason you name for choosing Google DeepMind is tied to something you actually
hold an opinion on, rather than a sentence that would fit any lab working on frontier AI.

Two-minute skeleton for "why Google DeepMind": about 20 seconds naming the one piece of the organisation's
public work — a model, a paper, a science result — you actually have an opinion on, not just approval of
it in the abstract; about 30 seconds connecting that to a concrete piece of your own history — a project,
a decision, the reason you moved toward this kind of work — that shows the interest predates this call;
about 40 seconds on what you would actually do in the role because of it; about 30 seconds on why now,
stated as a fact about your own situation rather than a complaint about your current employer. Keep the
whole answer under two minutes: longer starts to read as rehearsed rather than considered.

For "why leave / why now," state the reason as what you are moving toward, translating any real
dissatisfaction into the kind of work or environment you are seeking rather than narrating the complaint.
For the mission question, say it verbatim: Google DeepMind states its mission as "to build AI responsibly
to benefit humanity", and describes the team as "working to build the next generation of AI systems safely
and responsibly". Connect it to one concrete thing in your own work; not knowing the mission in its own
words is an easy way to lose the round. For the multiple-offers question,
name two or three criteria you would really weigh, in a stated priority order, and be honest that
compensation is one of them rather than pretending it isn't.

Most common way to lose points: a "why Google DeepMind" answer built entirely from the organisation's own
public description of itself — ambitious research, an important mission — with nothing that shows it is
about you specifically. Fix: before the call, write down the one piece of the organisation's work you
actually have an opinion on and the one piece of your own history that backs it up, and rehearse the
two-minute version until it fits without rushing.

### Research interests and team fit

What it probes: whether your stated interest is specific enough to be useful for matching you to a team,
and whether you understand what the role you are applying for actually involves day to day.

Skeleton, about a minute per question: name the one research or product area and the reason, tied to
something you have actually built, read, or used, not "it's an exciting time for AI". Phrase it around the
capability or problem you want to work on rather than a single team's name, so it reads as a direction
rather than a demand and does not rule you out of an adjacent team the recruiter might otherwise place you
on. For the ladder question, describe the ladder you are applying for as a difference in the shape of the
work — a research scientist role centres on research and publication, a research engineer role mixes
research with the engineering that gets a method running at scale, a software engineer role centres on
building and operating systems — and say which shape fits how you like to spend your time, rather than
which title sounds most senior. For the paper question, have the core idea in one sentence and a specific
reaction to it. For team openness, give a direct yes, no, or a stated condition, and name the one property
that would actually make a team a poor fit for you, rather than a vague "I'm flexible" that just becomes a
question the recruiter has to ask again later.

Most common way to lose points: describing the ladder you are applying for in terms of prestige rather than
fit — reaching for whichever title sounds more senior instead of the one that matches how you actually like
to divide your time — or naming a paper with no detail behind it, as if the title alone were the answer.
Fix: before the call, write the one honest sentence for why the ladder you chose fits your working style,
and have the paper's core idea and your reaction to it ready in two sentences.

### Background

What it probes: whether you can present your own experience concisely for a listener who will not follow
deep technical detail, and specifically how directly it lines up with the kind of work this role does.

Skeleton for the two-minute walkthrough: your current role in one sentence, then the single most relevant
project — what you built, your own part in it end to end, how long it took — then one sentence stating
plainly how much hands-on experience you have with the specific kind of work this role does day to day,
rather than leaving the interviewer to infer it from your title. If you are finishing a PhD, state the
thesis in one plain-language sentence understandable without your field's jargon, and give the expected
graduation date as a specific month rather than "sometime next year". Close with one sentence naming the
specific publication or project worth the team's time, and why.

Most common way to lose points: narrating a full career history instead of the one project that matches the
role, or a thesis explanation left in field-specific jargon that a non-specialist listener cannot follow.
Fix: before the call, write the plain-language, one-sentence version of the thesis or project, and say it
out loud to someone outside the field to check it actually lands.

### Logistics

What it probes: not a single correct answer, but whether your constraints are clear and workable enough for
the rest of the process to be scheduled around them.

**Work authorisation and visa.** State your status and any sponsorship need factually and early — this is
scheduling information, not a judgement of your fit, so there is no need to soften or over-explain it. A
constraint raised now costs less than the same constraint discovered after later rounds have been scheduled
around an assumption that turns out to be wrong.

**Location and hybrid.** Name the office locations that actually work for you, and say plainly how you feel
about the hybrid expectations for this role rather than agreeing to them in the moment and reopening the
question later.

**Start date.** Give a real date or a real window; for an internship, name the window explicitly, and for a
new graduate, give the expected graduation date rather than an approximate season.

**Other processes and deadlines.** State the stage each of your other processes has reached and any
decision deadline you have been given; there is no need to name the companies, since the stage and the
deadline are what scheduling actually needs.

**Level and compensation.** This question checks that a number is workable, not your true minimum. If the
level and the pay structure for the role — the split between base, equity, and any other components — have
not come up yet, ask about them before giving a number. Once you know the level being discussed, give a
range rather than a single figure, say plainly whether it is base or total compensation, and base the range
on research into the market for that role and level rather than on what you currently earn, which reflects
your last negotiation and not this role's worth. Pair the range with the level you believe you are
targeting, offered as your own assessment rather than a demand. If you would rather not commit to a number
yet, it is fine to say so and ask to revisit it once an offer is on the table, as long as you say so plainly
rather than deflecting the question.

**Interview language and accommodations.** If you want to interview in a language other than the default,
say so explicitly rather than assuming it will be arranged automatically. The same goes for any
accessibility need or accommodation: name it now, so it can be arranged before the round that needs it
rather than discovered on the day.

### Questions worth asking the recruiter

- What is the format and length of the rounds still ahead, and how many are there?
- Is this loop tied to a specific team already, or does team matching happen later — and if later, at what
  point in the process?
- Does the loop include a dedicated ML-knowledge round, a take-home exercise, or a presentation, and if so,
  at which stage?
- What level is the role being scoped at?
- What should I read or prepare before the next round?
- What is the expected timeline from here to a decision?
